How it began

Mom had an idea. We made it a family project.

Voxeloom started with Mom’s idea. Dad, our resident technology enthusiast, teamed up with CiCi and Didi to create the design studio. Each brought a different interest: art, technology, and a love of building with LEGO® bricks.

CiCi and Didi are our nicknames for big sister and little brother. Working together gives us a chance to learn from each other and make something we care about.

That’s the spirit we bring to your model, too. It might be the home where your family grew up, the place where you said “I do”, or a building that has always meant something to you. We want to understand the details that make it yours.

From your photograph to your model

A little imagination. A lot of checking.

Every LEGO® brick model starts as a grid — a voxel is a pixel with depth, and a house read off photographs is a lattice of coloured cells before it is anything else. Turning that into something a person can actually build is where the work is, and three things have to be true before a design leaves here.

Every piece is one LEGO sells

Not a piece that looks like it. Every part in a design is one LEGO sells new in its Pick a Brick shop, in a colour LEGO actually makes it in, and the whole parts list is checked against the shop before anybody sees the model. A part LEGO does not sell does not go in.

Every brick is held

A model is walked brick by brick to find anything resting on nothing. Where a piece floats, a support goes in underneath — and the change is written down and told to you rather than quietly shipped. If a design cannot be made to stand, you hear that instead of a box of pieces that will not hold together.

The instructions come out of the model

Every step and every numbered bag is generated from the finished design: nineteen thousand bricks sorted into build order in seconds. Nobody draws a booklet by hand here, which is most of the reason a kit takes weeks rather than seasons.

Made near Providence

Inspired by places close to home.

Five of the models in the gallery are within an hour of the workbench: the State House on its hill, Brown's Carrie Tower, Warwick Neck Light on its bluff, Beavertail Light at the southern end of Conanicut Island, and the Towers over Ocean Road in Narragansett; a little farther out, Rose Island Light keeps its old fort in the bay, a short ferry ride from Newport.

They were designed partly because they were there to look at, and partly because a building you have walked past a thousand times is the hardest thing to model. You know immediately when it is wrong.

If the place you want is in Rhode Island, say so — it is easier to get the details right when we can go and stand in front of it.
